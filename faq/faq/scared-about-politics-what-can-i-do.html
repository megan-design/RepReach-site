Taking Action

I'm Scared About What's Happening in Politics — What Can I Do?

Political fear is real, and it's appropriate when things that matter are at stake. Here's how to channel it into action instead of paralysis.

Your fear is valid — and useful

Fear Is a Signal, Not a Sentence

Feeling scared about political events isn't a weakness — it's a rational response to real stakes. When things that affect your healthcare, your rights, your family's safety, or your country's future are under threat, fear is the appropriate emotion. The question isn't whether to feel it, but what to do with it.

Fear has two modes: it can freeze you into paralysis, or it can mobilize you into action. The difference isn't about how scared you are — it's about having a clear path from the feeling to a concrete action. Helplessness turns fear into paralysis. Agency turns it into momentum.

The most important shift: From consuming information that amplifies your fear to taking actions that address it. These feel very different in your body. Doom-scrolling feels like doing something while doing nothing. Making a 30-second call to your senator's office feels like a small act but creates a sense of agency that compounds over time.

From fear to action

A Practical Path Through Political Anxiety

Limit your news consumption deliberately

You do not need to know about every political event as it happens. The 24-hour news cycle is not calibrated for your mental health or your civic effectiveness — it's calibrated for engagement, which means it systematically amplifies the most frightening stories. Give yourself permission to check the news once or twice a day instead of continuously. Your ability to act doesn't depend on knowing about every development in real time.

Convert fear into specific contact

When something scares you, look up who your representative is on that issue and make a call. It takes 30 seconds. You will feel measurably better afterward — not because you've solved the problem, but because you've done something real about it. The act of making contact transforms passive fear into active participation.

Focus on what's in your control

You cannot control Supreme Court decisions, executive orders, or what foreign governments do. You can control whether your representatives hear from you, whether you vote in every election, whether you stay informed about specific legislation, and whether you encourage others in your life to participate. That zone of control is real and consequential — focus your energy there.

Connect with people who share your concerns

Political anxiety is significantly worse when it's experienced in isolation. Finding community — whether through local advocacy groups, online spaces, or simply talking with friends who care about the same things — transforms an individual burden into a shared one. Shared action also tends to be more effective than individual action.

The deeper truth

Action Is the Antidote to Fear

The most consistent finding in research on political anxiety is that engagement reduces it. People who take civic action — even small, imperfect action — report lower levels of political anxiety than people who consume political news without acting on it. The act of participating gives you back a sense of agency that passive consumption takes away.

You don't have to fix everything. You don't have to be perfectly informed. You don't have to dedicate hours every week. You just have to do something — consistently, over time. That's enough. That's more than most people do. And in aggregate, it's how change happens.
