How Do I Contact My Representative in Congress?

Congress can seem like a distant institution. Here is exactly how to reach the people who work there on your behalf.

How Congress is structured

You Have Representation in Two Chambers

The U.S. Congress is made up of two chambers: the Senate and the House of Representatives. Every American who lives in a state has representation in both. You have one representative in the House (representing your specific congressional district) and two senators (representing your entire state). You can contact all three on any federal issue.

Key contact points:

• Washington D.C. office: Where your representative works when Congress is in session. Calls here reach the legislative staff who work on policy and constituent relations.

• District office: A local office in your home district. Often easier to reach during congressional recesses. Good for in-person visits and district-specific issues.

• Official website contact form: The standard channel for written constituent correspondence.

Step by step

How to Contact Your Congressional Representatives

1

Find your representatives

Go to house.gov and use the ZIP code finder for your House rep. Go to senate.gov and select your state for your two senators. Or enter your ZIP code in RepReach and see all three with contact info instantly.

2

Choose your contact method

Phone calls are the highest-impact regular contact method. Personal emails are second. In-person visits to district offices are the most impactful but less frequent. All are legitimate and all are tracked.

3

Prepare what you'll say

Your name, your city, the specific issue, and your position. For calls, 30 seconds. For emails, 3–5 sentences. RepReach generates a ready-made script so you never have to start from scratch.

4

Make contact

Call the office, submit the contact form, or visit in person. Your contact is logged. You're done.

5

Repeat — especially before key votes

Consistent contact over time is more impactful than a one-time spike. Timing contact before key votes maximizes its influence on the decision-making process.

What makes it effective

The Difference Between Contact That Gets Noticed and Contact That Doesn't

Congressional offices distinguish between genuine constituent correspondence and organized campaign contact. Your calls and personal emails carry more weight than form letters and petition signatures because they require individual effort and represent genuine constituent opinion rather than coordinated advocacy campaigns.

The constituent who calls once a week, on specific issues, and identifies themselves clearly as a voter in the district — that constituent is in a small minority. And that minority has disproportionate influence on the decisions their representatives make.
