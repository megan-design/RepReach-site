How Do I Contact My Representative?

You have three federal representatives and multiple ways to reach each of them. Here is the complete guide.

The basics

You Have Three Federal Representatives

Every American who is a U.S. citizen and lives in a state has three federal elected representatives: one member of the U.S. House of Representatives (representing your specific congressional district) and two U.S. Senators (representing your entire state). You can contact all three on any federal issue.

The fastest way to find your representatives: Enter your ZIP code in RepReach and your three representatives appear instantly — with their direct phone numbers, office addresses, and contact links. No research required.

Your contact options

Four Ways to Contact Your Representative

1. Phone call (most impactful)

Calling your representative's Washington D.C. office or local district office is consistently cited by former congressional staffers as the highest-impact form of constituent contact. It takes about 30 seconds. A staff member will answer, you state your name, your city, and your position on a specific issue, and hang up. That contact is logged and counted.

You can find direct phone numbers for all your representatives on their official .gov websites, or instantly through RepReach by entering your ZIP code.

2. Email or web contact form

Every congressional office has a contact form on their official website. A personalized email in your own words — explaining your position on a specific bill or issue and why it matters to you — is far more effective than a form email or petition. Keep it under 200 words: state who you are, where you live, what issue you're contacting about, and what you want your representative to do.

3. In-person visit to a district office

Every representative maintains district offices in their home district — not just in Washington. Visiting a local district office, or attending a town hall meeting the representative hosts, is the highest-impact form of constituent contact and signals an extraordinary level of engagement. You can find district office locations on representatives' official websites.

4. Written letter

Physical mail to a congressional office stands out because so few people send it. A handwritten or personally typed letter to your senator or representative — especially on an issue you feel deeply about — gets attention precisely because of its rarity and the effort it signals.

What to say

What to Include in Your Contact

1

Your name and location

State that you are a constituent — a resident of their district or state. This is the critical qualifier that establishes your contact as politically relevant to them.

2

The specific issue or bill

Be specific. "I'm calling about the reconciliation bill" or "I oppose the proposed Medicaid cuts" is more useful to the staffer logging your contact than general dissatisfaction with the direction of government.

3

Your position, clearly stated

Support or oppose. Ask your representative to vote yes or no. The clearer your position, the more useful the contact data is to the office tracking it.

4

A personal reason (optional but powerful)

If the issue affects you or someone you love personally — "I have a family member on Medicaid" or "I'm a teacher in a Title I school" — include it. Personal stakes make contacts more memorable and more likely to be noted beyond the tally.

Timing

When to Contact for Maximum Impact

Contact is most impactful in the days before a key committee vote or floor vote — not after the decision has been made. Staying informed about the congressional legislative calendar lets you time your contact strategically. RepReach tracks active legislation and upcoming votes so you always know when to act.
